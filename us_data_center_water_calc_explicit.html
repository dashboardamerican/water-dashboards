Disclosure gap — why some industries are missing

Several major US industrial water consumers (chemical manufacturing, steelmaking, mining, oil refining) are excluded because no current, primary-sourced consumptive-use data exists. The Census Bureau's Survey of Water Use in Manufacturing was defunded after 1983; USGS does not break out industrial water by subsector; and these industries do not voluntarily report consumption. Data centers are among the only US industrial water consumers with transparent, audited, annually reported water data.

RFS ethanol methodology & sources

This bar shows only the marginal irrigation water that would not exist without the Renewable Fuel Standard — not the total blue water embedded in all ethanol corn. Teter et al. (2018) modeled the Mandate scenario (RFS through 2030, ~15 Bgal/yr corn ethanol) vs. a no-biofuels counterfactual using coupled BEPAM economic + CropWatR biophysical models at 10 km resolution. The +729 billion liters/year net irrigation increase (≈ 192 Bgal/year) is concentrated in Kansas, Nebraska, Oklahoma, and Texas — regions that source >90% of their irrigation from the Ogallala Aquifer, a fossil groundwater system with negligible natural recharge. The same mandate also reduced groundwater infiltration by 587 billion liters/year, compounding the aquifer stress.

This is the most conservative estimate available: the total blue water of all US ethanol corn (1.0–1.4 trillion gal/yr before co-product allocation) is 5–7× larger. Convergent support: Lark et al. (PNAS 2022) found the RFS expanded corn acreage by 2.8 Mha with corn prices up ~31%; Sampson et al. (2021) showed ethanol markets drove ~4% of total Kansas irrigated water use at the field level, scaling consistently to the Teter national estimate.

Co-product note: DDGS (distillers grains) is a byproduct of ethanol, not the reverse — the RFS mandate drives the corn demand. Under energy-based LCA allocation, 57% of crop water is attributed to ethanol. The 192 Bgal marginal figure avoids the allocation question entirely by measuring only the net increase caused by the policy.

Food waste blue-water methodology & sources

This row represents blue water — surface and groundwater drawn for irrigation, under the Hoekstra/Mekonnen water-footprint definition — embedded in US food that is ultimately wasted at retail or consumer levels. Green water (rainfall used by rainfed crops) and grey water (pollution dilution) are deliberately excluded so the figure is comparable to the other irrigation-based rows in this chart.

Headline value — Conrad et al. (2018, PLoS ONE): 4.2 trillion gal/year (95% CI: 4.1–4.3T), computed by taking per-capita food waste from NHANES 2007–2014 dietary intake, disaggregating to ingredient-level cropland demand via the US Foodprint Model, then applying USDA-NASS irrigation rates by crop. The majority is concentrated in fruits (1.3T gal), vegetables (1.05T gal), and hay (1.01T gal) — reflecting both irrigation-intensive crops and high spoilage rates of fresh produce.

Convergent support — Birney et al. (2017, ERL) explicitly report ~54,000 L/person/year blue water in US food waste, which scales to ~13.9 km³/year (≈ 3,680 Bgal/year) for the US adult population — same order of magnitude. ReFED (2025) reports 16.2 trillion gallons of total fresh water (blue + green) for 2023 surplus food; the larger figure includes rainfall on rainfed crops and is not the same metric as the blue-water-only figure used here.

Why this belongs in the agriculture chart: food waste is an agricultural blue-water flow — it's the share of US irrigation that produces food we never eat. At ~4,200 Bgal/year, it is roughly 15% of total US crop-irrigation blue water (Conrad's figure / 28,600 Bgal/year USGS irrigation withdrawals).

  • Water method: procurement GW x capacity factor x 8.76 converts capacity to TWh/year; TWh x displaced thermal L/kWh estimates avoided thermal water and is capped at the no-hydro off-site baseline.

US food waste — embedded blue water

“An estimated 4.2 (95% CI: 4.1 to 4.3) trillion gallons of water [were] used annually to produce food that was wasted by US consumers from 2007 to 2014 ... the majority of wasted irrigation water was applied to cropland used to produce fruits (1.3 trillion gallons), vegetables (1.05 trillion gallons), and hay (1.01 trillion gallons).”

Conrad et al. (2018) PLoS ONE 13(4): e0195405. The figure is irrigation water from surface and groundwater, i.e. blue water under the Hoekstra/Mekonnen water-footprint definition (green water from rainfall and grey water from pollution dilution are not included). Derivation: per-capita food waste from NHANES 2007–2014 dietary intake → ingredient-level cropland demand via the US Foodprint Model → multiplied by USDA-NASS irrigation rates by crop. ReFED (2025) reports 16.2 trillion gallons of total fresh water (blue + green) for 2023 surplus food, a much larger figure that includes rainfall on rainfed crops and is not the same metric as the blue-water figure used here.
